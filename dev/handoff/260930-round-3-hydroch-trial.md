# Round 3 brief: a build trial from a published paper (30 September 2026)

From the maintainer's side (Claude Code, working with Graham Watt). Read `260930-round-2.md` first; its rules still apply. Round 2 (the accessibility fixes) has priority if it is unfinished. This trial is separate and lives entirely under `dev/`.

## TL;DR

- **The trial:** build a small new piece from a published, open paper, using only its public data and code, and report what worked and what didn't. It tests how far a session that sees only GitHub can get from a citation to a piece.
- **The paper:** Anderson, Astagneau, Gnann and Brunner (2026), "Reliance on daily mean streamflow data biases inferred flood seasonality", *Environmental Research Letters*, doi 10.1088/1748-9326/ae8e90. CC BY 4.0.
- **The idea:** *hear the same flood at two resolutions.* Daily-mean flow hides short, sharp floods; the hourly record shows them. The paper's own example is the Birse River in June 2021.
- **Where it goes:** `dev/trials/hydroch-resolution/`. Not `tracks/`, not `audio/`, no card, no lock entry. It is a trial, not a published track.
- **Limits:** no audio committed, no push to `main`, no changes to the site, engine or locks. Graham listens to anything you render; you can't, so verify by numbers and say so.

## 1. What is open and verified

I checked the licences through the Zenodo API on 30 September 2026.

| Item | Where | Licence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| The article | doi 10.1088/1748-9326/ae8e90 | CC BY 4.0 (stated in the article) | Open access on the publisher's site. |
| Analysis scripts | Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.18672062 | CC BY 4.0 | 10 KB zip, "functions and synthetic examples for high flow regime calculation and analysis". **Not** the full analysis. |
| Hourly discharge, HydroCH | Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.7691294 | CC BY 4.0 (on the Zenodo record) | 416 MB zip, about 291 catchments in Switzerland and neighbours. Files `*_Q_hourly.asc`, hourly values in UTC+1, an inventory spreadsheet `Inventory_discharge_hydroCH.xlsx` with coordinates in CH1903/LV03. |

**Not checked:** whether the Swiss federal agency behind HydroCH sets terms beyond the Zenodo label; the licence of CAMELS-CH (the catchment attributes, which you don't need). Note both in your report as unconfirmed.

**Not verified:** which HydroCH station is the Birse in the paper's example, and what the paper's figure shows exactly. Read the article for the station and the dates, then confirm against the inventory. Don't assume.

The article's abstract, quoted under its CC BY licence: "Floods are commonly analyzed using daily mean streamflow data, an approach that can obscure short-lived, high-intensity floods generated by sub-daily processes. Here, we examine how temporal resolution influences the inferred flood seasonality of peak flows. Using a regime-based framework, we extract flood events from both daily mean and daily maximum (aggregated from hourly streamflow) time series and smooth their frequency distribution to derive flood regimes." It finds the resolutions differ most in bi-modal snowmelt-and-rain regimes and in uni-modal summer-storm regimes, and differ little in strongly snow-influenced or winter-flood catchments.

## 2. What to build (a small piece, in stages)

**Stage 0. Read and plan (no code).** Read the article. Write a half-page plan in `dev/trials/hydroch-resolution/PLAN.md`: the question, the data you'll fetch, the mapping, the A/B options below with your recommendation. Commit it first, so the maintainer can redirect you early.

**Stage 1. Fetch, small.** A script `fetch_hydroch.py` that downloads only what you need from Zenodo (an inventory plus two or three station files, not the 416 MB zip if a partial fetch is possible; if not, fetch it, extract three files, and delete the rest). Print the checksums. Do not commit the raw data. Commit a **small derived extract** for the chosen stations only if it is under 5 MB, with the attribution in a README beside it; otherwise commit nothing and let the script fetch.

**Stage 2. The comparison, in numbers.** Reproduce one piece of the paper's finding with the public data and the Zenodo functions: for the Birse (or whichever station the paper's example uses), and one snowmelt-dominated and one rain-dominated station, show the daily mean against the daily maximum of the hourly series, and count how many flood events each resolution finds. Say what matched the paper and what you couldn't reproduce.

**Stage 3. The sound, from the engine, without samples.** Use `scripts/timegrid.py` (`Year`, `per_bar`, `norm01`, `smooth`), `scripts/harmony.py` (chords and modes) and `scripts/synth.py` (all synthesized, no instrument samples), the way `scripts/demo.py` does. The design rule of the whole project: **a slow part of the signal drives harmony; a fast part drives rhythm and texture. Never one literal value-to-pitch mapping.** Suggested mapping, which you may improve if you say why:
- **Slow:** the smoothed flow percentile of the catchment drives the chord and its mode.
- **Fast:** the hourly rate of change drives note or pulse density.
- **The comparison:** the same event heard twice, once from the daily mean and once from the hourly series, panned left and right or played one after the other. The listener should hear the flash flood appear in one and vanish in the other.

**A/B option for Graham (present both, recommend one, build the first):**
- **A. Side by side:** the two resolutions at the same time, left and right.
- **B. In sequence:** the daily-mean version, then the hourly version, with a short marker between.

**Stage 4. Verify without listening.** You can't listen. Check that the render is deterministic (same seed, same bytes twice), that its loudest passage falls on the event you meant, that the two versions differ where the numbers say they should, and that nothing clips. Report those numbers. Say plainly that a human has not heard it.

**Stage 5. Report.** A dated log in `dev/logs/` and `dev/trials/hydroch-resolution/REPORT.md`: what you built, what you reproduced from the paper and what you couldn't, what took longest, what was missing from this handoff, and what an unaided session would need to do this well. That last part is the point of the trial.

## 3. Rules for this trial

- **Everything under `dev/trials/hydroch-resolution/`,** plus your log. Don't edit anything outside `dev/`.
- **No rendered audio in git.** Write renders to `rendered/` (git-ignored) and report file sizes and checksums. If Graham wants to hear it, the maintainer will render it locally from your script.
- **Attribution and licence.** Everything you use is CC BY 4.0. Credit the four authors, the title, the journal and the DOI, the Zenodo records, and the fact that you changed the data (aggregated, extracted, sonified). Put that block in the trial's README. Do not choose a licence for the audio; write "proposed: CC BY 4.0, for Graham to confirm".
- **Voice.** Plain, active, concise, TL;DR first. Say who did what: Graham brought the question and the region interest; you wrote the code. Don't write as Graham.
- **Honesty.** Mark anything you couldn't verify. A trial that reports "I couldn't reproduce the paper's Figure X" is more useful than one that quietly substitutes something else.
- **Rules from round 2:** never push to `main`; no personal email addresses or absolute local file paths in anything you commit; no claims of listening or of screen-reader testing.
- **Scope:** if the data prove unworkable (the download fails, the format defeats you), stop at Stage 1 and write the report. A clean failure with the reasons is a good outcome.

## 4. Why this paper, for the record

It was picked from the maintainer's library by a search for open-licensed flood or hydrograph papers with public data and code. The runners-up (Basso et al. 2023 in *Nature Geoscience*, with code on HydroShare; Smith et al. 2026 on flood-frequency software; Pozo et al. 2026 on a hybrid flood model) were set aside because their data are spread across sources, or their licence wasn't confirmed, or they aren't time series you can hear. Anderson et al. has a hydrograph question, one open dataset, a small open code record and a clear sonification hook: the difference between two resolutions of the same river.
