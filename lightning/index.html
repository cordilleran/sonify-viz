Land acknowledgement (draft)

This storm passed over the unceded and ancestral territories of the Secwépemc, Tŝilhqot’in and Dakelh (Carrier) peoples, in the country now called the Cariboo and Chilcotin, and close to the lands of the Northern St’át’imc and Nlaka’pamux. We recognise everyone who has cared for this land, its waters, its ecosystems and its kin since time immemorial, and who care for them now, in a climate that is changing the land, the waters, the fire, and the storm.

About this page: sources and limits
  • Flashes. 2,424 optical flashes from the NOAA GOES-17 Geostationary Lightning Mapper, 30 June 21:43 to 1 July 05:24 UTC 2021 (14:43 to 22:24 PDT), inside 49.0 to 53.5°N and 125 to 118°W. The cluster runs up to the box's northern edge, so flashes farther north are not in this set.
  • Weather. Open-Meteo reanalysis at 35 points (sea-level pressure, wind at 10 and 100 m, temperature) and ERA5 pressure levels at the same points. Temperature is corrected with hourly readings from 81 Environment and Climate Change Canada stations: the correction is each station's difference from the reanalysis, spread to your pin by distance and faded away from stations. Held back one station at a time, the error falls from 4.5 C to 2.0 C. Valley-floor and mountain pins are the weakest; the correction does not know elevation.
  • The pin. By default it chases the storm: the densest cluster of the last 10 minutes, stood off 10 to 20 km on the left of its motion, so strikes arrive as distant flashes with direction. Drag the pin or click the map to take it back.
  • What you hear. Each flash heard within the reach (90 km by default) is a drum hit; the strings, flute, organ, tuba and bear sax follow the weather at the pin (see "How the data becomes sound"). The sound is a composed transform, not a recording of thunder. Real thunder carries tens of kilometres at most.
  • Not claimed. This page is about a night of lightning in a hot, dry summer, not about any one place or fire. It says nothing about what started any fire.
  • Height correction. The pin's ground height comes from a 2 km elevation grid (AWS Terrain Tiles). A lapse rate (the cooling of air with height) can lift or drop the reanalysis temperature from the height of the weather nodes to the ground under the pin: a fixed 6.5 C per km, a guess from humidity (dry air 9.8, saturated 4 to 6), one from the reanalysis profile, or one fitted to the stations hour by hour. Held out one station at a time (81 stations), none improves on the station correction alone: all five land within 0.13 C of each other, and before the station correction the three physical options make the reanalysis worse (raw error 6.0 to 7.7 C against 4.5 C with none). So the default is off and the lapse rate shown is the one you choose. The fitted option follows the stations as they are: in the afternoon the higher stations ran warmer than the reanalysis, the reverse of the textbook lapse rate, and in the evening they ran cooler. Treat it as a curiosity, not a measurement of the atmosphere.
  • Upper air. Wind and temperature at 850 hPa (about 1.5 km) and 700 hPa (about 3 km) come from the ERA5 pressure-level reanalysis (Copernicus). They draw the wind rose and temperature path; they do not yet drive any sound.

How the data becomes sound
  • Each flash is a drum hit on a 1/8-second grid. The strongest flash heard in each eighth-second also gets a crack when it is hard enough and within 45 km, softer and duller the farther it is. Distance picks the drum (under 12 km log drum, to 30 km conga or tumba, to 60 km bass drum or timpani, beyond that low timpani), is a lag (about 3.5 ms per km, capped at 0.3 s, plus a small fixed random looseness; real sound takes 2.9 s per km), and dulls the sound. Optical energy sets how hard it is hit. Direction sets left and right. Big hits drag a duller echo behind them, and the biggest ring a gong.
  • Dense moments: three hits at most per step; the rest of a crowded step is heard as a shaker (rain on the drum heads), and five or more flashes in one step bring a quiet low roll (at most every 12 s).
  • Harmony has three versions to compare. B (default) holds the third back and sounds a suspended 2nd, so the harmony neither says major nor minor; temperature still moves the colour note (Lydian when hot, Dorian otherwise). A follows temperature through D Lydian (the hottest third of this storm's pin temperatures), Dorian (the middle) and Aeolian (the coolest third); the page prints the limits when it loads. C alternates F♯ and F, 24 s a turn, stays in Dorian, and lifts the cello an octave.
  • Surface wind picks the cello's note from the mode (stronger wind, higher degree), one change at most every 4 s. Wind at 100 m above 12 km/h breathes a low flute. Wind shear brings the organ.
  • Events are set from this storm's own changes along the chase (the page says the numbers when it loads): the tuba sounds on a sea-level pressure fall in the last 30 min and also swells continuously with the rate of fall; the bear sax sounds on a surface wind change or a gust rise in the last 30 min. At most one of each every 8 s.
  • The drone (contrabass, cello D and A) never changes: it is the ground the changes are heard against.
