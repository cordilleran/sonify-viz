The wheel: eight organ notes

The order and pace never change: one note a week, a full turn every eight weeks. As the total ice shrinks, the highest and lowest notes (top) fall silent first and the middle notes (bottom) hold longest. Dashed rings are silent notes. Pale halos are the upper octave that open water adds.

How it's made

One bar is one ice year. Each bar is 16 seconds, October to September, 48 bars from 1979 to 2026, and every week of ice is one pulse.

The wheel. Eight organ notes turn in the same order at the same pace, one note a week: C, G, C, E♭, F, G, B♭, C. They come from a minor pentatonic scale, which has no semitones and no tritone (the harshest intervals), so whichever notes are left, none of them clash in that way.

The ice decides how many can sound. In a full March all eight; in the 1980s Septembers, six; in the 2012 record low, two. A note that drops out is a rest, not a quieter note, so the loop keeps its length and pace, and the notes that remain keep the pitch and touch they had in 1979. The highest and lowest go first and the middle ones hold longest, so the loop gets a gap that grows and the range narrows toward the middle. The loss follows the ice as a whole, not particular seas.

The cello is thickness. It plays one note every two months, and its register follows ice volume (from a model): thick ice is low and warm, thin ice is high and bare, so over the record it climbs. Each note swells in with a bow instead of a strike.

The bass is the floor. A low C sits under everything while the ice is thick, and it is gone by the 2020s. The cello and bass also rise and fall with each year's extent.

Open water is light. When the sea opens, the organ gains its upper octave and the room grows wider and brighter. Thinning and opening are told apart: the cello says how thick the ice is; the organ and the room say how much water is open.

Time bends a little. Weeks press slightly in freeze-up and drag in melt, but every bar is exactly 16 seconds. After the last bar come six October days of refreeze: the wheel restarts thin, the cello holds an open fifth, and it is left unresolved. The method is that of process music, except that the process here is subtraction.

What this picture tells, and what it does not

The wheel and the voices panel are drawn from the rules that made the sound. The number of organ notes follows total ice extent, a satellite record (R). The cello's register and the bass follow ice volume, which is a model (M). Warming is not in this piece; the Round, the main piece of the pair, will carry it. Land snow, green and melt are a model (M) run at 29 points, about 650 km apart: they show the timing of the seasons, not local detail, and are not a satellite product. Days with no satellite grid repeat the nearest one. The disc at the pole is not measured. The satellites' orbits do not pass over the pole, so each sensor leaves a round area unseen: about 600 km in radius until August 1987, about 305 km until the end of 2007, about 95 km until the end of 2024 and about 140 km since (measured from the grids used here). The map draws it as full ice inside a dashed circle, and NSIDC's extent figures count it as ice too. The disc shrinks in 1987 and 2008 because the sensors changed, not the ice. The extent record starts on 26 October 1978, so the first weeks of the 1979 bar repeat that day's value. Before mid-1987 extent was recorded every other day, and there are no data from 2 December 1987 to 13 January 1988; those days are interpolated.
